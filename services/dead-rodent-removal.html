Dead Rodent Removal

A dead rodent announces itself on a predictable timeline. Within a day or two of death, a faint sweet-sour smell appears; by days three to five it becomes intense as decomposition peaks, often with flies gathering at windows, and it can linger for two to four weeks in a warm home before fading. The smell is strongest near the carcass, which usually sits where the animal sheltered: a wall void, attic corner, chimney breast or crawl space. Murvo's dead rodent removal starts with that smell map, narrowing the location before any opening is made. It is often needed after rodent trapping or poisoning by another provider has left animals to die indoors.

Location is a process, not guesswork. Technicians work methodically, checking the warmest and quietest voids first, because carcasses are usually found close to the harborage the animal used while alive. Technicians compare odor strength room by room, check likely voids with inspection tools, and use access from attics or crawl spaces wherever possible to avoid cutting into walls unnecessarily. Our wall rodent removal experience means any opening made is small, targeted and made good afterwards. The carcass is removed wearing PPE, double-bagged and disposed of properly, then the area receives rodent disinfection and, where the smell has soaked in, rodent odor removal treatment. Dead rodent removal is quoted as a flat rate in writing, reflecting how accessible the carcass is — an attic retrieval differs from a wall-void extraction. As context, Angi's 2026 national averages put rat extermination at $396, generally $189–$655, with inspections at $100–$200, and locating a carcass is essentially skilled inspection work. We also address why the rodent died there: if it was poisoned, we will recommend trap-based rodent control instead, so animals are recovered rather than lost in voids.
